# Petals, Patterns, and Predictions: A Random Forest Model to Forecast the Peak Cherry Blossom Bloom Dates from Global Climate Data

### Abstract

Predicting peak cherry blossom bloom dates is essential for tourism, bioclimatic modeling, and phenological research. Cherry blossom peak bloom is highly sensitive to countless biological factors that are both local and global. Peak bloom is influenced by complex interactions among temperatures, weather oscillation patterns, dormancy requirements, and long-term climate change. This study develops a data-driven random forest model that combines several climatological parameters to estimate the cherry blossom peak bloom timing for five different cities: Washington, D.C.; Kyoto, Japan; Liestal, Switzerland; Vancouver, Canada; and New York City for 2026.    

Historical peak bloom data through 2025 were combined with daily meteorological records collected from NOAA, NASA, and AccuWeather web sources. Information derived from these sources that were the primary phenological predictors were, daily maximum and minimum temperatures, precipitation, and solar radiation. In addition, other factors that were also primary predictors were growing degree days and chilling accumulation, which were calculated from the variables listed above. To further predict the phenological response to warming, global CO2 concentrations and global temperature anomalies, along with the El Nino-Southern Oscillation trends and the Oceanic Nino Index. Given the historical depth of meteorological records incorporated, there were extensive gaps in the early years documented. The discontinuities were addressed using interpolation that filled missing data, which upheld atmospheric dynamic patterns while preserving stochastic variance.        

When training the random forest model, the daily biological predictors were aggregated into annual summaries for every location. Since the data for 2026 is incomplete, the short-term temperature forecasts were manually integrated for February through mid-April. The global climate indicators, specifically CO2 concentrations, global temperature anomalies, El Nino-Southern Oscillation trends, and the Oceanic Nino Index were extrapolated based on the most recent patterns. This allowed the model to function using projected environmental scenarios without relying on latent data points. 

Random forests were selected for the capability to model nonlinear relationships and interactions without stringent parametric assumptions. To reduce the risk of extreme outliers and improbable predictions, using each city’s most recent ten-year peak bloom window, a constraint was applied to ensure that the forecast remained realistic with examined historical fluctuations. 
This model provides a resulting location-based peak bloom date predictions for 2026 that considers both local weather variations and global climate impacts. Analyzing variable importance, it highlights global temperature anomalies, CO2 concentrations, and El Nino-Southern Oscillation trends as the most dominant predictors. Overall, this model demonstrates how combining long-term atmospheric signatures with biological threshold parameters can advance the robustness of phenological projections. This modeling framework is flexible and can be extended for future applications to other species, locations, and climates as meteorological fluctuations continue to shape seasonal biological events.
